Trials that end in "above" are typically those with broad, pragmatic enrollment criteria—such as anatomical or spatial qualifiers, pregnancy restrictions, and numeric thresholds for lab values—that aim to capture a real-world, generalizable patient population while still maintaining objective, measurable eligibility standards. In contrast, trials that end in "below" are characterized by strict, disease-specific exclusions (e.g., neurological or CNS conditions), explicit treatment sequences, and detailed post-treatment monitoring or protocol-section references, which narrow the population and impose rigid procedural control that may reduce generalizability or introduce complexity. The key mechanism is the balance between inclusivity and operational rigidity: above-outcome trials prioritize feasibility and broad applicability, while below-outcome trials prioritize internal validity and strict protocol adherence, often at the cost of real-world relevance. To predict the outcome of a new protocol, a reader should first check whether the eligibility criteria emphasize broad anatomical or spatial qualifiers and numeric thresholds (favoring "above") versus explicit exclusions for neurological conditions and detailed treatment-strategy specifications (favoring "below").
